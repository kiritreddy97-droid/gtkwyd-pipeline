## Hotter than the Sun
The air inside a lightning channel can reach about 30,000 degrees Celsius for a
fraction of a second. The surface of the Sun is around 5,500 degrees, so a bolt is
roughly five times hotter.

## Thunder is the sound of air exploding
That intense heat makes the air expand faster than the speed of sound, creating a
shockwave. The crack you hear is that shockwave. The rumble is the same sound
arriving from different parts of a long bolt.

## The bolt goes both ways
A faint channel steps down from the cloud, but the bright flash you see is a return
stroke racing back up from the ground at up to a third of the speed of light.

## It strikes the same place often
The old saying is wrong. Tall isolated objects like the Empire State Building are
hit dozens of times a year, because height and a good path to ground are exactly
what lightning seeks.
[[skyscraper in storm]] [[lightning rod on building]]

## Most lightning never reaches the ground
The majority of strikes happen inside or between clouds. Ground strikes are the
minority, even though they get all the attention.

## It makes a rare glass
When lightning hits sand or certain soils, the heat can fuse it into a hollow glassy
tube called a fulgurite, a permanent cast of the bolt's final path underground.
