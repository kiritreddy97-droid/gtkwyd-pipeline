## The old plug problem
Older USB plugs only worked one way round, so you often had to flip the cable
and try again. USB-C was designed from the start to be reversible, so either
side plugs in correctly.
[[usb c cable close up]] [[person plugging in cable]]

## Way more than one job
Inside the same small connector, USB-C can carry regular data, high-speed
video signals, and enough electrical power to charge a laptop, all through
identical looking pins.
[[usb c ports laptop]] [[cable connected to laptop]]

## The cable and the port negotiate
When you plug in, tiny chips in the cable and the device exchange signals to
agree on how much power to send and what kind of data will flow, all within a
fraction of a second.
[[circuit chip macro]] [[charging cable connected]]

## Not all cables are equal
Two USB-C cables can look identical but support very different speeds and
power levels internally, which is why some chargers work fine while others
charge painfully slowly with a cheaper cable.
[[multiple usb c cables]] [[charging speed comparison]]

## One port, many standards
The same physical USB-C shape can carry entirely different underlying
technologies depending on the device, from basic charging to full external
monitor connections.
[[monitor connected via usb c]] [[usb c hub devices]]

## Why regulators pushed for it
Because a single standard cable can charge phones, tablets, and laptops
alike, some regions have required manufacturers to adopt USB-C specifically
to cut down on electronic cable waste.
