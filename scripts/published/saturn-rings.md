## Mostly ice
Saturn's rings are made almost entirely of water ice, from grains the size of dust
to chunks the size of houses. The ice reflects sunlight well, which is why the
rings are so bright through a telescope.
[[saturn rings telescope]] [[planet saturn space]]

## Wide but incredibly thin
The main rings stretch about 280,000 kilometres across, yet in most places they are
only about ten metres thick. Scaled to the width of a football field, they would be
far thinner than a razor blade.

## They may be young
Evidence from the Cassini spacecraft suggests the rings could be only a few hundred
million years old, meaning dinosaurs on Earth may have lived before Saturn had
rings at all.
[[cassini spacecraft saturn]] [[dinosaur silhouette sunset]]

## Saturn is slowly eating them
Ring material is drifting into the planet as a faint dust rain pulled down by
gravity and Saturn's magnetic field. On astronomical timescales the rings are
disappearing.
[[saturn atmosphere storm]] [[rings casting shadow on saturn]]

## The gaps are carved by moons
The dark divisions in the rings are not empty by accident. Small moons and
gravitational nudges from larger ones sweep certain lanes clear, keeping the gaps
open.
[[saturn moon among rings]] [[close up saturn ring gaps]]

## Every giant planet has rings
Jupiter, Uranus, and Neptune all have ring systems too. They are just faint and
dark, made of dust rather than bright ice, so Saturn gets all the attention.
