## Two events in a fifth of a second
A hiccup starts with a sudden spasm of the diaphragm, pulling air in. About a
quarter of a second later the vocal cords snap shut, cutting it off with the
characteristic sound.

## The nerves involved are long
The reflex runs along the phrenic and vagus nerves, which travel a long, exposed
path through the neck and chest. Irritation anywhere along them can trigger a
bout.
[[nervous system illustration]] [[neck and chest anatomy]]

## Common triggers
Eating too fast, fizzy drinks, a sudden temperature change, and stress or
excitement are all frequently linked to short-lived hiccups.
[[glass of soda bubbles]] [[person eating quickly]]

## An evolutionary echo
One idea is that the hiccup reflex is related to the gill-ventilation motions of
fish and the gulping of air by amphibians like tadpoles, reusing ancient nerve
circuitry.
[[tadpole in water]] [[fish gills close up]]

## Most fixes work by resetting breathing
Holding your breath or breathing into a bag raises carbon dioxide, which seems to
calm the reflex. Many folk remedies simply interrupt the breathing rhythm.
[[person holding breath]] [[breathing exercise]]

## The record is decades long
One American man reportedly hiccuped almost continuously for 68 years, an extreme
case usually caused by nerve damage or irritation.
