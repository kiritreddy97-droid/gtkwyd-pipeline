## The year does not divide evenly
Earth takes about 365.2422 days to orbit the Sun. A calendar of exactly 365 days
drifts by almost a quarter day every year.
[[earth orbiting sun]] [[calendar pages]]

## Adding a day catches up
Adding February 29 every four years covers most of that gap. This was the fix
introduced by the Julian calendar in 46 BC.
[[february 29 calendar]] [[roman calendar illustration]]

## But four years is slightly too much
Adding a full day every four years overcorrects a little. Over centuries the
Julian calendar slipped until the spring date had wandered by about 10 days by
the 1500s.
[[old astronomical clock]] [[seasonal drift illustration]]

## The century rule
The Gregorian calendar, adopted from 1582, drops the leap day in years divisible
by 100 but keeps it in years divisible by 400. So 1700, 1800, and 1900 were
normal years, and 2000 was a leap year.
[[gregorian calendar document]] [[year 2000 celebration]]

## It is now very accurate
This system is off by only about one day in 3,000 years, close enough that no
further fix is planned.
[[modern wall calendar]] [[clock and calendar]]

## Leap seconds are separate
Occasionally a leap second is added to clocks to match tiny changes in Earth's
rotation. That is a different adjustment from the leap year.
