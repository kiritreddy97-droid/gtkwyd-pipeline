## The Earth's orbit is not a perfect circle
Your calendar system is based on the Earth's orbit around the sun, but it's not entirely accurate due to its elliptical shape. This variation affects the amount of solar energy the Earth receives, causing the seasons to change. The Earth's orbit is actually an ellipse, with the closest point (perihelion) occurring around early January and the farthest point (aphelion) occurring around early July.

## A year is approximately 365.24 days long
The Earth takes approximately 365.24 days to complete one orbit around the sun. This is the basis for our calendar year. However, if we were to have a calendar with exactly 365.24 days, it would drift away from the actual solar year over time, causing problems for our seasons and weather patterns.

## Julius Caesar introduces the Julian calendar
In 45 BCE, Julius Caesar introduced the Julian calendar, which was the first solar calendar to account for the Earth's orbit. The Julian calendar had a leap year every four years, but this resulted in an average year length of 365.25 days, which is close to the actual solar year length.

## A mistake is made, but it's not disastrous
The Julian calendar's leap year rule resulted in an average year length of 365.25 days, which is about 11 minutes longer than the actual solar year length. This might not seem like a lot, but over centuries, it added up. However, the difference was not catastrophic, and the calendar remained in use for many centuries.

## Pope Gregory XIII corrects the mistake
In 1582 CE, Pope Gregory XIII introduced the Gregorian calendar, which refined the Julian calendar's leap year rule to eliminate the error. The new rule dropped three leap years every 400 years, which resulted in an average year length of 365.2425 days, much closer to the actual solar year length.

## The Gregorian calendar is the one we use today
The Gregorian calendar is the one we use today, and it's the reason we have leap years every four years, except for years that are divisible by 100 but not by 400. This rule ensures that our calendar stays aligned with the Earth's orbit around the sun.
